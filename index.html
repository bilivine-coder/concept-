Before the First Pipe: Where Approvals Slow Site Down

In MEP and construction delivery, a great deal of the schedule depends on things that are not on site at all — material approvals, method statements, welder qualification, ITP sign-offs. When those are tracked carefully, the sequence holds. When they are tracked in parallel spreadsheets, across offices and time zones, the sequence quietly slips.

The sequence that has to happen first

Before installation begins on most critical systems, a chain of approvals must be complete — pipe and material approvals, storage and prefabrication planning, WPS/PQR and welder qualification, welding samples through RT and bend tests, method statement and ITP approval. Only then does production start.

A familiar observation

Each step in that chain is routine. What makes the chain fragile is that it depends on several parties — the consultant, the supplier, the technical office, the QA/QC team — responding in sequence.

"One missing item can stop an entire installation sequence."

Where the friction tends to sit

In businesses with technical offices in more than one location, three patterns appear often — usually without anyone deciding to create them.

01

Status held in many places

The register lives in a spreadsheet. Updates live in email threads and messages. The site team gets a different answer depending on who they ask.

02

Chasing depends on individuals

Following up with a consultant or supplier tends to sit with whoever remembers. When that person is on leave or overloaded, items go quiet.

03

Rejections lose their trail

An item returned with comments is easy to lose. Without a visible resubmission date, it can sit for weeks before anyone notices.

A concrete pattern: the approval that goes quiet

The same scenario plays out across most project businesses. The information exists. What is less clear is where it currently sits and who is holding it.

Scenario: A material submittal awaiting consultant response.
The status is knowable. The visibility is not automatic.

How it often works

1 Submittal sent to the consultant by email.
2 Register updated by hand in one office.
3 A follow-up is sent when someone remembers.
4 Comments come back to an individual's inbox.
5 Site asks for status. Answer depends on who is asked.

What coordination looks like

1 Each submittal logged once, with owner and due date.
2 The register is the shared source of truth across offices.
3 Reminders go out on a set cadence, not by memory.
4 Rejections logged with comments and a new due date.
5 A weekly summary shows approved, stuck, and at risk.

A lightweight approach

Not a new platform. Not a replacement for existing systems. A thin layer that keeps approvals visible and moving, and takes the chasing out of individual inboxes.

Step 1

Log once

Every submittal recorded in one register — item, discipline, owner, due date, current holder.

Step 2

Chase on schedule

Reminders sent to the consultant or supplier on a fixed cadence. Escalation when there is no response.

Step 3

Track the loop

Rejected items carry their comments and a resubmission date, so nothing returns to the inbox and disappears.

Step 4

Summarise weekly

One page each week: approved, awaiting response, at risk of affecting site sequence. Generated, not assembled.

Adjacent workflows this tends to touch

Once approvals are visible, the same pattern often applies nearby — usually as a second step, not the first.

Supplier quote comparison

RFQ responses reviewed side by side on compliance, price and lead time, rather than comparing PDFs by hand.

BOQ and scope gaps

Items required by specification but missing from the BOQ, and accessories not shown on drawings — flagged before pricing.

Drawing and BIM deliverables

Shop drawings and BIM packages tracked on the same register, so the technical office has one view across locations.

What this is — and what it isn't

Not a replacement for the ERP or existing systems.
Not another dashboard to maintain.
Not an automation of engineering judgement.
Not a new tool for site teams to learn.

It is a focused layer that reduces the manual effort between an approval being needed and an approval being closed — with the decisions remaining with the consultants, reviewers and engineers accountable for them.

How a pilot would be measured

Any change of this kind should be judged on outcome rather than activity. A focused pilot can baseline and track:

Approval cycle timeFrom submission to close, by discipline.
Items going quietSubmittals with no movement beyond the due date.
Manual effortTime spent on status checks and follow-ups.
Site impactApprovals flagged before they affect the sequence.
The question worth asking

Where does the largest gap sit between what the technical office already knows about a submittal — and what the site team can see when they need to plan? And can that gap be closed without adding headcount or replacing the systems already in place?
